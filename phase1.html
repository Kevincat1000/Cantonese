17th–19th Centuries

Early Migration within Southeast Asia

1640s–1770s: Early Migration to Siam

Following the Qing dynasty's conquest of South China (c. 1644–1660s), significant Cantonese migration occurred. Ming loyalists and refugees fled south by sea to escape warfare, while widespread corruption and hardship under Qing rule compelled many farmers and artisans to seek livelihoods abroad.

Maritime routes linked Guangdong's coastal communities to Siam (Thailand), Cambodia, and Cochinchina, where local rulers welcomed Cantonese traders and skilled workers for their commercial and fiscal value.

By 1700, the Chinese population in Ayutthaya reportedly exceeded 3,000. This community expanded significantly throughout the 18th century, becoming prominent in royal monopolies, navigation, and commerce.

1680s–1790s: Establishment in Southern Vietnam

Around 1680, Mo Jiu, a refugee from Leizhou, Guangdong, developed the Hà Tiên area. He formally established a semi-autonomous principality there in 1708. This functioned as a sanctuary for Cantonese migrants.

During the Tây Sơn conflicts, Cantonese merchants founded a new settlement adjacent to Saigon in 1778, which evolved into Chợ Lớn ("Big Market"). Despite severe persecution in 1782, survivors rebuilt the community. By the late 1700s, the Cantonese population was estimated at 30,000–40,000.

1786–1820s: Expansion into British Colonial Ports

From the late 18th century, British colonial ports reshaped Guangdong migration routes. Penang (est. 1786) offered security that attracted migrants. The Ghee Hin Kongsi emerged as a major brotherhood, organizing labor and mutual aid.

Following the establishment of Singapore in 1819, Cantonese migrants integrated into the colony. In 1822, Cho Ah Chee established the Ning Yeung Wui Kuan, marking a pivotal moment of institutional consolidation for Singapore's Cantonese community.

1840s–1870s: The Malayan Tin Boom

Open-cast Tin Mine in Malaya, 1900s

With the discovery of rich tin deposits in Perak, Selangor, and Negeri Sembilan, a new wave of migration occurred. Cantonese laborers and capitalists moved from Penang and Singapore into the interior mining districts.

Summary: Social Mobility

By the turn of the 19th century, the Cantonese population occupied a unique niche. Through brotherhoods and clan associations, they organized labor and capital. By the late 19th century, a distinct class of magnates emerged, controlling tin mines and revenue farms.
