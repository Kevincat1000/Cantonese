Phase III: 1965–1997

Reforms, Refugees, and New Waves

1965–1967: Opening Doors and New Professionals

The mid-1960s was a major turning point for Cantonese migration to North America. In the United States, the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart–Celler Act) ended the discriminatory quota system, shifting the focus to family reunions and skilled labor. Canada moved in a similar direction; after removing race-based rules, Ottawa introduced a "points system" in 1967 that prioritized education, language skills, and job potential.

These policies changed who was coming. While early migration was mostly rural laborers from Sze Yup, this new era allowed the arrival of urban, educated, and professional Cantonese people. This shifted the community from a society of single male laborers to a growing middle class with strong global connections.

1975–1980s: The Refugee Crisis and Commercial Revitalization

The end of the Vietnam War and the rise of communist rule triggered a massive humanitarian crisis. Within this chaos, the Hoa (ethnic Chinese in Vietnam), most of whom spoke Cantonese, faced severe political pressure and the seizure of their businesses. Beginning in 1978, hundreds of thousands fled as "boat people" to camps in Southeast Asia. The United States became a primary destination, admitting a significant number of these refugees. It is estimated that by 1990, approximately 200,000 ethnic Chinese from Vietnam had settled in the U.S., with the majority being Cantonese speakers from the Chợ Lớn area of Saigon.

The arrival of the Hoa was critical for North American Asian communities. They did not just settle in existing neighborhoods; they revitalized them. Many older Chinatowns, such as those in Los Angeles, were facing decline, but these refugees brought new money, labor, and business energy that saved them. Furthermore, they sparked the creation of entirely new commercial zones. In Southern California, for example, while the broader Vietnamese community established the "Little Saigon" district in Westminster, many of the foundational businesses, supermarkets, jewelry shops, and trading firms were actually started by Cantonese-speaking Hoa. Acting as a bridge between older Cantonese networks and the newly arrived Southeast Asian population, this group transformed areas like the "Little Saigon" district in Westminster (California) and Toronto's Spadina Avenue into thriving hubs, extending the reach of Cantonese commerce far beyond its traditional borders.

1984–1997: The Hong Kong Migration and Suburban Growth

Following the 1984 agreement that scheduled the return of Hong Kong to China for 1997, a massive migration wave occurred due to political uncertainty. Estimates suggest over 500,000 Hong Kong residents left during this period, with Canada and the United States serving as top destinations.

Unlike previous groups, these migrants were often wealthy professionals and investors. They skipped the traditional Chinatowns, settling instead in suburban areas such as Monterey Park (California), Richmond (British Columbia), and Markham (Ontario). This created suburban Chinese centers known for their wealth and international ties. This wave established Cantonese as a major language of international business and shifted the community's center from historic labor districts to modern, globally connected suburbs.
