01. Hegemony

The Era of Hegemony, 1850s–1970s

Early Cantonese dominance was not just about numbers—it was about institutional power. In the 19th and 20th-century Chinatowns, native-place associations (huiguan) acted like a government. They dominated trades, settled disputes, and provided welfare.

Cantonese was the price of admission. Without it, an immigrant could not borrow money, find a job, or access legal protection. The community’s support system was effectively closed to anyone who did not speak the language.

I.W. Taber, “3546 The New Chinese Joss House, San Francisco,” c.1891.

02. Displacement

Diglossia and Displacement

Sociolinguists describe “diglossia” as a system where a “High” language is used for official business and a “Low” language for family life. Mandarin is increasingly treated as the “High” variety—official, educated, and globally useful. Meanwhile, Cantonese is pushed toward the “Low” variety.

Heritage education is a primary site where this hierarchy is produced. In many schools, Mandarin is enforced as the only "proper" identity, rendering other repertoires invisible.

03. The Reversal

The Great Reversal

Canada’s 2021 Census confirmed Mandarin as the leading Chinese language (~679,000 speakers), surpassing the aging Cantonese demographic. In the U.S., Mandarin surpassed Cantonese around 2010, though Cantonese remains vital for institutional access in legacy Chinatowns like San Francisco.

04. Identity

Identity and Resistance

If Cantonese is no longer the dominant economic credential, its value shifts to Authenticity. It becomes a marker of regional roots, Hong Kong identity, and diasporic history. The emergence of a "Cantonese Renaissance" among youth activists suggests a conscious effort to preserve the language for cultural continuity rather than economic utility.
