B. Media Hegemony: The Public Voice

Cantonese dominance was also reproduced through media institutions. San Francisco's early Chinese press was written exclusively for a Cantonese-speaking labor class.

The Golden Hills' News (1854), the first Chinese newspaper in the U.S., circulated among early migrants. In the early 20th century, major papers such as Chung Sai Yat Po (1900–1951) shaped political and civic discourse for decades, also spelled in Cantonese. Even when these newspapers discussed modern issues, they spoke to a Cantonese world.

The media did not just reflect the community; it defined the boundaries. By making Cantonese the default language of news and opinion, these papers established who was "inside" the community's political imagination and which linguistic groups were peripheral.

C. Surnames and Signs: The "Standard Sound"

A third layer of evidence comes from the linguistic landscape: the signs and names that defined Chinatown. In many older Chinatowns, English spellings of surnames and store names historically tracked Cantonese pronunciations: "Lee," "Wong," and "Chan" became the norm, rather than the Mandarin pinyin "Li," "Wang," or "Chen."

This was more than just spelling. It signaled that Cantonese was the "standard sound" of public life. In the eyes of the community and the broader American public, Cantonese was not just a dialect; it was the accepted standard for being Chinese in North America.
