1848–1858 The North American Gold Rushes

The discovery of gold in California triggered the first major wave of Cantonese migration to North America. Between 1848 and 1851, approximately 25,000 migrants arrived, sailing primarily from the Sze Yup (Four Counties) region. San Francisco served as the primary entry point, where Cantonese migrants established the continent's first Chinatown. This district functioned not only as a transit hub for newcomers but also as North America's earliest permanent Cantonese settlement.

In 1858, the discovery of gold in British Columbia drew the migration northward. Cantonese miners, arriving from California or directly from southern China, founded Victoria's Chinatown. Known as the "Gateway to Canada," it remains the oldest Chinese enclave in the country.

1850s–1900s The Plantation Economy in Hawaii

A distinct wave of Cantonese migration transformed Hawaii, beginning with contract laborers in 1852. Unlike the Sze Yup-dominated mainland, the majority of Hawaii's community was Heungshan (Zhongshan), comprising roughly 75% of the Chinese population.

Between 1852 and 1899, approximately 46,000 migrants arrived. By 1884, they constituted 22% of the total population and held 49% of plantation jobs, forming the backbone of the sugar industry. By the 1890s, they also dominated rice cultivation, successfully transferring South China's agricultural technology to the Pacific.

1865–1869 The Transcontinental Railroad

Ging Cui, Wong Fook, and Lee Shao, three of the eight Chinese workers who put the last rail in place, on a float at the 50th Anniversary celebration of the completion of the transcontinental railroad in Ogden, Utah. Photo by courtesy of Amon Carter Museum of American Art Archives, Fort Worth, TX

During the construction of the Central Pacific Railroad, more than 12,000 Chinese laborers were recruited. Roughly 90% were Cantonese migrants from Guangdong, mostly from the Sze Yup region. These workers formed the backbone of the project.

Estimates suggest that several hundred to over one thousand Cantonese laborers died, often due to extreme cold, avalanches, and blasting accidents. From 1863 to 1869, the Central Pacific completed roughly 690 miles of railroad from Sacramento to Promontory Summit, joining the Union Pacific on May 10, 1869. The contributions of these Cantonese laborers were pivotal in accelerating U.S. economic integration and national expansion.

1868 Diplomacy and Free Migration

The Burlingame Treaty (1868) marked a high point in early U.S.–Qing relations. It affirmed reciprocal Qing's most-favored-nation treatment in travel and residence. Most importantly, it recognized the mutual advantage of free migration for trade, travel, or permanent residence while condemning coerced migration.

In this diplomatic context, the Qing government soon launched initiatives such as the Chinese Educational Mission (1872–1881). More broadly, the treaty's language of "free migration" helped legitimize and protect transpacific mobility in the late 1860s and early 1870s. Combined with labor demand and established Cantonese migrant networks, it reinforced the United States as a major destination for Cantonese workers until the agreement was later curtailed and ultimately undermined by restrictive policies in the 1880s.

In 1872, 120 selected children were sent to the United States, and about 70% of them came from Guangdong Province.
The earliest surviving remittance letter from the United States. Jiangmen Museum.
Letter from 1871

In this 1871 letter from San Francisco, Zhencong explains why he left Xinning/Taishan, Guangdong: poverty at home pushed him to go abroad to earn a living and secure a better future. He reassures his elder brother that he is safe, reports receiving news that the family is well, and most importantly, sends money (a $20 silver draft) for rice and daily necessities, carefully listing earlier remittances and the messengers who carried them.

The message highlights a central motive behind many Cantonese migrations to the United States: working overseas to support the household through remittances, rather than simply seeking personal adventure or permanent departure. It also reflects strong filial piety: duties to parents, and a sense that an individual's success abroad is measured by what he can send back and how he honors the family.

1882–1943 Exclusion, Resistance, and Renewal

Following the completion of the Pacific Railroad, economic shifts fueled hostility toward Cantonese labor. This culminated in the U.S. Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the first federal law banning a specific ethnic group. Canada followed with the 1885 Head Tax and the restrictive Chinese Immigration Act of 1923. These policies severed ties between Cantonese laborers and their hometowns and banned the entry of women, transforming North American Chinatowns into "Bachelor Societies" void of traditional family structures.

The community challenged these restrictions through legal channels. In 1898, Wong Kim Ark, a Cantonese descendant born in San Francisco, appealed his denied entry to the Supreme Court. The ruling in United States v. Wong Kim Ark affirmed birthright citizenship (jus soli), securing the legal status of American-born Cantonese and facilitating the transition from temporary sojourners to permanent citizens.

Wong Kim Ark Document, 1898

World War II marked a turning point for Chinese Americans. Acknowledging China as an ally, the U.S. repealed exclusion act via the Magnuson Act (1943), though it imposed a restrictive annual quota of 105. Canada followed in 1947, restoring voting rights.

The War Brides Act of 1945 allowed the entry of Cantonese women. This demographic shift ended the "Bachelor Society" era, re-established family units, and revitalized Chinatowns as centers of Cantonese commerce and culture for future generations.
