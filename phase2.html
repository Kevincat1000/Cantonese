Building North America & The Pacific

The North American Gold Rushes

The discovery of gold in California triggered the first major wave of Cantonese migration to North America. Between 1848 and 1851, approximately 25,000 migrants arrived, sailing primarily from the Sze Yup (Four Counties) region. San Francisco served as the primary entry point, where Cantonese migrants established the continent’s first Chinatown.

In 1858, the discovery of gold in British Columbia drew the migration northward. Cantonese miners, arriving from California or directly from southern China, founded Victoria’s Chinatown—the oldest Chinese enclave in Canada.

Miners at Auburn Ravine, California, 1852

The Plantation Economy & The Iron Road

Hawaii: Beginning with contract laborers in 1852, a distinct wave of Cantonese migration (mostly from Zhongshan) transformed Hawaii. By 1884, they constituted 22% of the population, dominating sugar and rice cultivation.

Railroad: Between 1865 and 1869, over 12,000 Cantonese laborers built the Central Pacific Railroad. Working through extreme conditions in the Sierra Nevada, they were instrumental in connecting the American continent, though hundreds lost their lives in the process.

Diplomacy and Remittance

The Burlingame Treaty (1868) initially protected free migration. This era also saw the Chinese Educational Mission (1872), where boys from Guangdong were sent to study at Yale and Columbia.

Letter from San Francisco, 1871

In this letter, a migrant named Zhencong explains his departure from Taishan was driven by poverty. He sends a $20 silver draft back home for rice—a testament to the "Remittance Economy" where Cantonese success abroad was measured by the survival of the family back in China.

Exclusion and Legal Resistance

The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act and Canada's 1923 Immigration Act created "Bachelor Societies." However, the community fought back in court.

In 1898, Wong Kim Ark, born in San Francisco to Cantonese parents, won a Supreme Court landmark case. The ruling affirmed jus soli (birthright citizenship), ensuring that American-born Cantonese were citizens, not "aliens ineligible for citizenship."

Summary: A Lasting Legacy

Through a century of exclusion and labor, Cantonese migrants laid the physical, legal, and commercial foundations of modern North America. They transformed from "sojourners" into permanent citizens, leaving an indelible mark on the Pacific frontier.
